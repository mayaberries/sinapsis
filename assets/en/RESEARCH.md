# Pain Points of Health Professionals Using Generic Tools for Remote and Hybrid Care — with a Psychology-First Lens (Mexico/LatAm first, US second)

The biggest pain is not video quality. It is that generic tools were never built around the "encuadre" (the therapeutic frame), so practitioners rebuild it by hand every day, out of WhatsApp, Zoom/Meet links, Google Forms, email and spreadsheets. In Mexico and LatAm, WhatsApp is the real clinical channel, and it is wearing down boundaries and fragmenting the clinical record. In the US, practitioners already have telehealth-specific platforms but complain about juggling apps and about the limits of video for couples, families and children. Psychologists and therapists carry a heavier version of every pain. Their work depends on rapport, privacy, between-session contact and psychometric follow-up, and generic tools handle all four badly.

## TL;DR

- **In LatAm, WhatsApp is the consulting room, and it is eating practitioners' time and boundaries.** FUNSALUD's survey of more than 2,000 Mexican physicians found instant messaging is the digital tool they use most (79%). Only 32% use specialized videoconferencing platforms at least weekly. Psychologists describe messages at 9:30 pm on a Sunday, and calling it a "carga invisible" (invisible load) has become common. The fix is not "more video". It is a structured, boundaried relationship channel that patients already find as easy as WhatsApp.
- **For therapists, the core breakages are the frame, privacy at the patient's end, and clinical work that video doesn't support.** That means family listening in, sessions from cars, unstable connections, the burden of setting up and closing the session, crisis moments with no plan, and couples, family and child work squeezed onto one screen. In a 491-clinician LatAm survey, technology problems and domestic interruptions were the main difficulties. A 183-clinician US survey rated engaging young children (8.03/10) and youth speaking freely at home (6.60/10) as the top barriers.
- **Documentation and information exchange is the overlooked opportunity.** Practitioners email PDFs, run PHQ-9/GAD-7 through Google Forms, score by hand, store results in spreadsheets, and send worksheets, receipts and reports through different channels. The highest-value product move is a single patient-practitioner "shared space" around each session: before, during and after. It would hold questionnaires, worksheets, summaries, uploaded files and admin artifacts, deliberately staying out of e-prescription and regulated-record territory.

## Key Findings

1. **WhatsApp is the dominant channel between practitioners and patients in Mexico, ahead of any video tool.** FUNSALUD's "Médic@ Digital en México" study found 79% of doctors use instant messaging (WhatsApp, Telegram, Messenger) and 67% use phone calls. Only 32% used specialized videoconference platforms at least once a week, and 34% rarely did. Its 2024 edition reports 50% of surveyed doctors do teleconsultations, and names WhatsApp as the main doctor-patient channel. On the patient side, FUNSALUD's "Paciente Digital Mexicano" study found 57% of people use messaging apps for health consultations, with WhatsApp the main channel to professionals.
2. **The cost is practitioners' rest and boundaries.** Expansión (February 2026) headlined that 70% of Mexican doctors attend patients by WhatsApp "a expensas de su descanso". Note that the 70% figure comes from the CEO of Leona, a WhatsApp-integrated vendor, so treat it as directional rather than audited. In Argentina, a pediatrician was reported to receive around 50 WhatsApp messages a day, with photos and audio "sin reconocer día ni horario". Disclaimers in WhatsApp status ("if it's urgent, go to the ER") "rarely" stop them.
3. **Psychologists face a clinically loaded version of the same problem.** Out-of-hours messages are not only admin. Some are attachment-driven, such as nighttime requests for reassurance. Therapists are advised to treat contact policy as a clinical intervention: written policy, verbal review, consistent auto-replies and fixed reply windows. On Doctoralia México, psychologists told a patient that messaging rules should be set in the informed consent, and that answering every patient "podría volverse emocionalmente abrumador".
4. **In LatAm, the home is both the therapist's and the patient's consulting room, and it leaks.** A survey of 491 mental-health workers (92.9% psychologists; 67.3% in Argentina, the rest elsewhere in Latin America) found 92.87% delivered online care and 91.45% worked from home. Technology (signal drops, frozen images, incoming-call interruptions) and domestic noise and interruptions were the main difficulties. Respondents worked over WhatsApp, Google Meet or Zoom. In a Colombian university clinic, 35.1% of trainee therapists reported problems with the patient's physical space. Patients' main complaint was getting a stable, easy internet connection.
5. **Rapport survives, but practitioners must work harder to get it.** In the Colombian study, 85.7% of patients said a trusting relationship was possible "always" or "almost always" online. LatAm practitioner blogs still consistently report losing nonverbal information, a "colder" first contact, and harder alliance building, particularly with patients who have communication difficulties.
6. **US psychologists are mostly hybrid, and they don't find telehealth easier on themselves.** APA's 2024 Practitioner Pulse Survey (n=846) shows 19% fully remote, 69% hybrid and 12% fully in person; its December 2025 edition reports 21% remote, 65% hybrid and 14% in person. Earlier APA data found 47% did not agree that telehealth is less emotionally taxing than face-to-face, against 23% who thought it was. Hybrid is the norm, so tools must handle both in-person and video sessions for the same patient.
7. **Couples, family and child work break the "one face per tile" model.** Family therapists found it "almost overwhelming… to have them all on one screen". They struggled to soften conflict without nonverbal cues, or to ask partners to move closer. In a 183-clinician survey, the top barriers were engaging young children (8.03/10) and youth talking freely at home (6.60). Using worksheets in session (5.89), technical performance (5.34) and administering standardized assessments (4.49) followed, and telehealth sessions took longer to prepare.
8. **Fatigue is real and specific to video.** Counselors report "listening exhaustion" from back-to-back telehealth sessions, partly because nonverbal cues are missing. The IBE Ludwigshafen "Zoom-Fatigue" study (Rump & Brandt, 2020), cited in a psychotherapy journal, found around 60% of 422 respondents felt Zoom fatigue. An EEG/ECG study of 35 students (Riedl et al., Scientific Reports, 2023) found "significant changes in physiological and subjective fatigue" after 50 minutes of videoconferencing. Self-view is a known contributor.
9. **Fragmentation is the cross-market constant.** Even inside purpose-built US systems, therapists complain about "too many clicks", calendars that don't sync ("I cannot create an appointment in iCal and have it upload"), no simple way to email clients, and "back and forth" between scheduler and notes. A 14-therapist CSCW (2021) study concluded that generic platforms made it hard to sustain both alliance and interventions.
10. **Psychometrics are handled informally.** Practitioner-facing guides list the typical errors: distributing copyrighted tests (BDI-II is licensed; PHQ-9, GAD-7 and DASS-21 are public) through Google Forms, keeping results in unencrypted spreadsheets, and not verifying who answered. The good-practice model is to send the PHQ-9/GAD-7 before each session and review a trend graph with the patient. Generic tools don't support that.

## Details by Theme

### A. Mexico and Latin America (Priority 1)

**1. WhatsApp: boundaries, time and the relationship**
- *What happens:* WhatsApp handles booking, rescheduling, reminders, "quick questions", lab-result photos, voice notes, payment proofs and emotional check-ins. One Colombia/Mexico-focused vendor estimates 30–60 non-therapy WhatsApp messages a week for a typical caseload. A single reschedule takes 6–10 messages. These are vendor estimates and plausible, not audited.
- *Why it hurts:* Replying after hours "implicitly communicates that limits are flexible". Interruptions break concentration between sessions. A Spain-based psychologist on Threads described replying briefly and picking the issue up in session, and commenters noted therapists may have "muchísimos pacientes que igual les mandan mensajes a cada rato".
- *Relationship damage (medicine):* Halabe-Cherem and Hamui-Sutton (Medicina Interna de México, 2018) argue WhatsApp consultation shortens the dialogue further and is "not the ideal means" of care. Some Mexican doctors respond with signs reading "no doy consultas por WhatsApp". The authors still concede WhatsApp is useful for small things, like a dosing question after a visit or a brand-name drug missing at the pharmacy. Spanish primary-care commentary adds that WhatsApp consultations lack traceability: impressions never reach the record.
- *Why practitioners still use it:* Patients find WhatsApp easier than a formal telemedicine booking. Some therapists deliberately leave WhatsApp open after a first session to lower resistance to starting therapy. Even a Doctoralia-using specialist in Spain falls back to WhatsApp video "only when everything else fails". **Implication: practitioners don't want to ban WhatsApp-style contact. They want it bounded, structured and connected to the clinical relationship.**
- *Evidence that structured messaging can be therapeutic:* A randomized trial of 850 adults on Talkspace (Pullmann, Areán et al., JAMA Network Open, October 2025), covered by El Español, found "no differences between groups on improvement in depression or social functioning" between message-based and video therapy at 12 weeks, with therapists replying between 15 minutes and 14 hours later. The commentary stresses that it only works with defined frequency, response times and goals: "No vale con intercambiar mensajes al azar".

**2. Connectivity, devices and patient-side friction**
- Unstable internet is the main patient complaint (Colombia). Practitioners name signal drops, frozen images and incoming calls interrupting the session as the main technical obstacles (Argentina/LatAm).
- Many patients are mobile-only and on WhatsApp by default. Asking them to install Zoom, create a Google account or open a link in a desktop browser adds friction, and practitioners end up falling back to WhatsApp video calls. Spanish clinicians say online therapy is a poor fit when the patient "doesn't manage computers, email, video platforms, online payments" or has poor internet. That describes many older and low-digital-literacy patients.
- *Caveat:* I couldn't retrieve recent hard data on LatAm older-adult digital literacy. Treat this point as practitioner-reported and well documented qualitatively.

**3. Privacy perception and emotional safety at home**
- Practitioners report patients "se cortan" (hold back) when they know their partner is moving around the house. Online therapy is considered unsuitable when "the home situation is complicated and there is barely any privacy".
- In video care, providing a private, comfortable space "is no longer the psychologist's exclusive responsibility", and therapists have no tools to help patients set one up.
- For therapists working from home, noise, children, pets and doorbells were the top home-office difficulties in the 491-clinician LatAm survey.

**4. Session flow and the therapeutic frame**
- Psychologists with a "rigorous encuadre" and no online experience found the shift especially hard (Argentina/LatAm). Generic tools offer no sense of a session: no waiting room tied to the appointment, no time awareness or gentle overrun cue, and no structured opening or closing.
- No-shows and late cancellations are handled by manual WhatsApp chasing. Doctoralia's own figures (vendor data) say 43% of online bookings happen outside working hours and 35% of calls to clinics go unanswered. That shows demand arrives when practitioners aren't available.

**5. Documentation and information exchange (LatAm specifics)**
- *Psychometrics:* Spanish-language PHQ-9 versions are validated in Mexico (55,555 teachers; α=0.89) and Colombia (cut-off ≥7). Practitioners still send PDFs or Google Forms and score by hand. Vendors such as Kalyo and Gesell sell automatic scoring, item-9 (suicidality) alerts and trend graphs, which confirms the manual baseline is painful.
- *Materials:* 26% of Colombian trainee therapists reported difficulty with materials needed for certain therapeutic modalities online. Worksheets, drawings and exercises don't transfer well over Meet or WhatsApp.
- *Results and documents from medicine:* In Mexico, large networks like Salud Digna use WhatsApp to deliver results. In private practice, patients send photos of lab results, prescriptions and studies into chats, where they get buried.
- *Receipts and invoicing (workflow only):* In Mexico, patients can ask for a CFDI and practitioners must collect RFC, full name, postal code and tax regime. They usually chase this over WhatsApp after the session and re-type it into a separate invoicing tool. Payment proofs (bank transfer screenshots) also arrive by WhatsApp. "Dificultades en el cobro de honorarios" (transfers, insurers) appeared as a named obstacle in the LatAm clinician survey.

**6. Fatigue**
- "Cansancio visual" (eye strain) and "mayor agotamiento" (greater exhaustion) were among the obstacles LatAm clinicians were asked about and reported.

### B. United States and English-speaking markets (Priority 2)

**1. Platform maturity, but fragmentation**
- US therapists widely use purpose-built tools like Doxy.me and SimplePractice. Doxy.me reviewers praise the persistent personal link and the virtual waiting room, and wish they could "put a consent form in my waiting room". SimplePractice users complain about clicks, calendar sync and moving between scheduler and notes. **Implication: the US bar is already "waiting room + same link + no download". Differentiation must come from relationship and workflow, not basic video.**

**2. Patient environment**
- Patients join from cars. Talkspace explicitly advises parked cars for privacy, and California Board of Psychology survey respondents list "My car" and "car during work lunch" as session locations. California rules require confirming the client's location at each session, which is a protocol matter we won't build on. In practice it means therapists need a quick, natural way to know where the patient is and who is around.

**3. Crisis mid-session**
- Teaching cases describe clients disclosing suicidal intent and then ending the call. Assessment gets harder "when clients abruptly leave a session or end a call". Generic tools give the therapist nothing at that moment: no pre-agreed emergency contact or local resources, and no way to reconnect by phone.

**4. Couples, families, groups, children**
- Some couples join on one screen, others from separate locations. Families get "squeezed together". Therapists lose leaning in, tone modulation and in-room choreography. Children don't engage in talk-only video, and engagement-focused platforms (e.g., Teleo, now in an NIMH-funded trial against standard videoconferencing) are emerging. Group sessions raise others-can-hear-you problems when members don't use headphones.

**5. Fatigue and emotional load**
- Back-to-back video produces listening exhaustion, and self-view and nonverbal overload drive Zoom fatigue. US psychologists don't see telehealth as less taxing (47% vs 23%).

### C. Market differences at a glance

| Dimension | Mexico / LatAm | US / English-speaking |
|---|---|---|
| Default channel | WhatsApp (chat, voice, video) | Telehealth platform + EHR + SMS/email |
| Core pain | Boundaries, invisible admin load, no traceability | App fragmentation, clicks, sync |
| Connectivity | Unstable; mobile-only patients common | Generally stable; car sessions common |
| Admin workflow | Manual CFDI data chase, transfer screenshots | Insurance billing, portals |
| Psychometrics | PDFs/Google Forms, hand scoring | Built-in measures in some EHRs, but siloed |
| Therapy specifics | Strong "encuadre" culture; relational, psychodynamic and systemic traditions | Measurement-based care norms; MFT and child platforms emerging |

## Recommendations: Feature and Opportunity Ideas (non-technical, prioritized)

**Tier 1: Build first (highest pain, psychology-first, low regulatory exposure)**

1. **"Encuadre" channel: a boundaried messaging space instead of personal WhatsApp.** Patients write as easily as on WhatsApp, but inside the practitioner's frame. Visible "office hours", a warm auto-reply written by the therapist, an agreed response window ("I reply Mon–Fri within 24 h"), and a clear "this is not for emergencies" path with local crisis numbers. Messages are grouped per patient, and the therapist can choose to bring a message into the next session agenda ("let's talk about this on Thursday"). *Addresses:* after-hours messages, invisible load, attachment-driven contact, lost traceability. *Psychology-specific:* the contact policy becomes part of the therapeutic contract and can be reviewed in session.
2. **Session "before–during–after" shared space.** Every appointment gets one page both people see. Before: reminder, a one-tap join that works on mobile, a short "how are you arriving today?" check-in and the pre-session questionnaire. During: a shared notes or whiteboard area and screen-shared worksheets. After: a short practitioner-written summary, "homework" for the week and the next appointment. *Addresses:* fragmentation, homework loss, frame setup, worksheets over email.
3. **Psychometric follow-up made human.** A library of public-domain questionnaires (PHQ-9, GAD-7 and similar) sent automatically before chosen sessions, with automatic totals and a simple trend chart the therapist can show the patient as a conversation tool. A gentle flag to the therapist when a risk item is endorsed is a clinical-attention cue, not an automated response. Licensed instruments (e.g., BDI-II) only via the publisher's channels. *Addresses:* Google Forms and hand scoring, spreadsheets, no progress visibility. Keep the feature framed as "monitoring support", never diagnosis.
4. **Low-bandwidth, mobile-first, "it just works" joining.** No installs or accounts, works on low-end Android phones, an audio-only fallback that keeps the session "alive", and automatic rejoin plus a one-tap "call me by phone" if the video drops. *Addresses:* LatAm connectivity, mobile-only and older patients, abrupt drops that break emotional moments.
5. **Privacy-at-home helpers for patients.** A pre-session "find your space" nudge, headphone suggestion and a discreet "safe word / I'm not alone" signal the patient can send the therapist without speaking (e.g., a private button or emoji). Plus an easy switch to chat-in-session if someone walks in. *Addresses:* family nearby, patients holding back, car sessions.

**Tier 2: Strong differentiators**

6. **Session-rhythm aids for the therapist.** A quiet time-remaining indicator only the therapist sees, a "wrap-up" cue, and optional hiding of self-view to cut fatigue. A private side-notepad that doesn't make the therapist look away from the patient. *Addresses:* overruns, Zoom fatigue, note-taking during video.
7. **Crisis-ready sessions.** Before the first session, the patient records their usual location and an emergency contact in their own profile, and the therapist keeps local crisis lines per patient. If a call drops at a delicate moment, a one-tap "reconnect / call back" plus a gentle follow-up message template. This is support for the therapist's own judgment, not a protocol engine. *Addresses:* crisis mid-session, abrupt hang-ups.
8. **Couples, family and group modes.** Join from one device or several, with a layout that keeps all members visible, a "speaker turn" view, a private sidebar for individual check-ins, and "everyone use headphones" prompts for groups. *Addresses:* one-screen squeeze, conflict de-escalation, group privacy.
9. **Child and play-friendly room.** A shared drawing board, simple digital play objects, and a caregiver co-facilitator view (e.g., caregiver coaching overlay). *Addresses:* child engagement, which US clinicians rate as their top barrier.
10. **Patient document inbox (instead of chat photos).** Patients upload lab results, prior reports, school letters and studies into their own folder, tagged and dated. Practitioners share their own documents (reports, referral letters, treatment-plan summaries, worksheets, prescriptions they issued in their approved systems) as attachments. The platform just delivers and organizes; it doesn't generate or validate prescriptions. *Addresses:* buried WhatsApp images, emailed PDFs, Drive links.

**Tier 3: Workflow glue (admin, non-price)**

11. **Admin "close the loop" after each session.** Automatically ask the patient once, at onboarding, for receipt or invoice details (in Mexico, the CFDI data fields) and payment proof, stored with the patient and exportable to whatever invoicing tool the practitioner already uses. *Addresses:* manual CFDI data chasing and lost transfer screenshots, without becoming an invoicing or tax product.
12. **Calendar honesty.** Two-way sync with Google/Outlook/iCal, self-rescheduling within the practitioner's rules, and reminders over WhatsApp templates and email. *Addresses:* 6–10-message reschedules, no-shows, sync complaints.
13. **Supervision and interpreter seats.** Invite a supervisor (observer, audio-muted) or an interpreter into a session with a clearly visible indicator to the patient. *Addresses:* training-clinic workflows (common in LatAm university clinics) and multilingual care.

**What to avoid (to stay out of regulated territory):** no e-prescription generation or validation, no claim to be the legal clinical record (expediente clínico), no automated diagnosis or risk scoring that triggers actions, and no tax-compliant invoice issuing. Integrate with or export to the tools practitioners already have approved.

## Caveats

- **Reddit and social-media evidence:** Despite targeted searches (including a dedicated sub-investigation), no Reddit threads, in English or Spanish, surfaced through the available search tools. This reflects a tool limitation, not proof of absence. First-hand voice here comes from a Threads post, Doctoralia Q&A, Capterra/G2 reviews, survey free text and peer-reviewed qualitative studies. Before building, validate with direct interviews and by mining Reddit and Facebook groups (e.g., Mexican and Argentine psychologists' groups), which need logins.
- **Vendor-sourced figures:** The "70% of Mexican doctors use WhatsApp" figure (Leona CEO), the 30–60 messages a week (Asistchat) and the Doctoralia booking and call statistics come from companies selling solutions. They are directional. FUNSALUD's 79% messaging figure is the most robust Mexican datapoint.
- **Pandemic-era data:** Several LatAm surveys (2020–2021) reflect emergency conditions. Domestic interruptions were amplified by lockdowns, though the structural issues (WhatsApp dependence, connectivity, privacy at home) persist.
- **Medicine vs. psychology:** Mexican quantitative data on WhatsApp use is strongest for physicians. Evidence for psychologists is mostly qualitative and from practitioner literature.
